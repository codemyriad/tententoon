ten·ten·toon /ˌtɛn.tɛnˈtoːn/ noun

1. a self-repeating image whose copies spiral as they shrink, in the manner of M. C. Escher's Print Gallery.

• a tententoon of the old gallery, winding inward without end.

Origin coined from the Dutch Prentententoonstelling (“print exhibition”): prenten, prints + tentoonstelling, exhibition. The coined word sits right where the two halves meet.

What if outside and inside had no border?

A picture that contains a smaller copy of itself is called the Droste effect, after the 1904 Dutch cocoa tin whose nurse carries a tray bearing the very same tin. You can fall inward forever — yet every copy stays fenced inside its own frame, cleanly separating the world outside from the miniature world within.

What if that boundary dissolved? Follow the painted wreath around the portal below: instead of closing into a separate ring, the frame itself spirals inward, carrying the outer room seamlessly into the smaller room inside it. Here is the four-step machine that weaves them together.
